# 6.4.0 benchmark, 2026-11-12

6.4.0 adds the reader level and limits the status tag to reports on work.
This run asks three things: whether replies got worse or dearer for someone
who sets no level, whether `new` produces replies a newcomer can follow, and
what that costs.

## Result

The shipped 6.4.0 covers more key points than 6.3.1 on every model, at
about the same clarity and error rate. Sixteen questions: the original
eight and eight written afterwards (below). The ± is one standard error.

| | Opus | Sonnet | Haiku | All |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Key points covered, 6.3.1 (72 replies a model) | 78.5% ±1.8 | 78.1% ±2.0 | 75.2% ±2.2 | 77.3% ±1.1 |
| Key points covered, 6.4.0 (32 replies a model) | 81.2% ±2.2 | 85.2% ±2.1 | 80.5% ±2.4 | 82.3% ±1.3 |
| Clarity (1 to 5), 6.3.1 and 6.4.0 | 4.26, 4.25 | 4.31, 4.31 | 4.38, 4.44 | 4.31, 4.33 |
| Factual errors per reply | 0.76, 0.69 | 0.46, 0.38 | 0.54, 0.50 | 0.59, 0.52 |
| Cost of a cold call, 6.4.0 against 6.3.1 | +5% | +5% | +6% | |

The gain has a price. Replies are about 8% longer (median 755 against 698
characters on the original questions, 853 against 784 on the new ones),
more of them run over the 600 budget in a single cold call, and the style
text is 4,325 characters against 3,434, which is what adds the 5% to a
cold call. Over the whole suite an earlier draft of the same rule saved
31%, 8% and 4% against default on Opus, Sonnet and Haiku, where 6.3.1 saved
35%, 11% and 5%; with two runs per conversation that 3-point gap is inside
the noise, and the shipped text wasn't rerun on conversations.

### How the coverage rule came about

The first 6.4.0 drafts covered no more than 6.3.1 (sections below). Point
by point, both missed the same kinds of things that default covers: what
else to check in a diagnosis, the decisions in a plan, the cost of a
recommendation, the way back from a risky step. A rate-limiting plan named
an algorithm in 6% of replies against 83% for default. The new rule lists
what each kind of reply must contain and asks for one clause per further
point, with a closing `Also check:` line.

That rule was written after reading this run's rubric, so it was tested on
eight questions written afterwards, with a rubric Opus wrote from those
questions alone: authentication against authorization, a container killed
with exit code 137, REST against GraphQL, adding a non-nullable column to a
large table, a config-reader review, undoing a pushed commit, a slow
indexed query, and moving cron jobs off one server. On those, the rule
covered 84.6% ±2.0 against 79.9% ±1.9 for 6.3.1; on the original eight,
79.9% ±1.6 against 76.0% ±1.4.

With `new` and the shipped style, 48 replies covered 74.5% ±2.1 and scored
3.1 of 5 for a newcomer, at 17% more per cold call than 6.3.1.

The sections below record the earlier drafts. All runs together: 1,260
conversations, 1,650 turns, $74.53 at API-rate estimates, and $20.09 for
grading 1,128 replies.

## Method

Headless Claude Code with `--plugin-dir`, on Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5 and Haiku
5.5. The arms are the default style, 6.3.1, 6.4.0 with no level, 6.4.0 with
`new`, and 6.4.0 with `expert`. The cases are `bench/ab.py`'s eight questions,
a depth question, a PR-description draft, the slugify coding task and the
three six-turn conversations, each run twice. The eight questions then ran
four more times for 6.3.1 and 6.4.0. In all, 804 conversations and 1,104
turns cost $52.89 at API-rate estimates, and grading cost $12.92.

The October harness and its rubric were lost with a temp folder, so both
were rebuilt. Opus wrote eight key points per question from the questions
alone ([rubric](data/bench-2026-11-12-rubric.json)). Sonnet graded each
reply in its own call, with the status tag stripped and no arm named.
Coverage here is stricter than October's and can't be compared with it,
which is why 6.3.1 ran again as an arm. Besides coverage, the grader scored
how well a person new to programming could follow the reply (1 to 5) and
listed the technical terms it used without explaining.

Two things changed during the run, and each changed arm ran again:
- The first `new` note told Claude never to drop explanations and gave 900
  characters. Replies explained more and covered 60% of key points. The
  shipped note says to simplify the wording and keep the substance, with
  1,100 characters.
- The first style told Claude to judge the reader's level when none was set.
  It explained terms to everyone and covered 72% against 6.3.1's 76%. The
  shipped style assumes a working developer unless the reader's words show
  otherwise.

## Reader levels, before the coverage rule

Eight questions. `new` is the shipped note, 32 replies per model; `expert`
is 16.

| | Opus | Sonnet | Haiku |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A newcomer could follow it (1 to 5), no level | 2.1 | 2.1 | 2.2 |
| A newcomer could follow it, `new` | 3.1 | 3.0 | 2.8 |
| Unexplained terms per reply, no level and `new` | 12.0, 9.8 | 11.8, 10.0 | 12.8, 10.6 |
| Key points covered, `new` | 71% ±3 | 71% ±3 | 69% ±3 |
| Key points covered, `expert` | 75% ±3 | 84% ±4 | 74% ±4 |
| Median reply prose, `new` | 1,020 | 999 | 1,157 |
| Cost of a cold call, `new` against no level | +7% | +11% | +18% |

`new` moves the newcomer score up by about one point of five and still
leaves about ten terms a reply unexplained, so a newcomer gets a more
readable answer, not a fully plain one. It covers 4 points less than no
level and costs 7 to 18% more per call. With the first note, over the
ops conversation and the single-turn cases, `new` cost 22% less than default
on Opus, 1% less on Sonnet and 7% more on Haiku; the shipped note allows
longer replies and wasn't run on conversations.

`some` and a level in the reader's own words were not benchmarked. Depth
answers under the first `new` note were about half as long on Sonnet (4,400
to 4,700 characters against 8,200 to 8,800); the shipped note says an ask
for depth still lifts the budget, and that wasn't rerun.
